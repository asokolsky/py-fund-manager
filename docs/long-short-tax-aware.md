# long-short tax-aware Strategy

Hong Kim, a PhD in economics, helped AQR develop an innovative tax optimization strategy before leaving to found Quantinno. With thousands of wealth managers and their ultra-high-net-worth clients, the company's assets grew from $2 billion to $70 billion in just three years. Forbes USA reports on the details.

The stock market boom has dragged on, creating a tax problem for the wealthiest Americans: investment profits are high, but losses are virtually nonexistent. Major indexes continue to rise, and tech company founders and executives are accumulating wealth in concentrated stock holdings. It's becoming increasingly difficult for the wealthy to identify unprofitable assets that would otherwise generate losses and offset their capital gains.

A strategy that addresses this problem is gaining popularity and has already become a hit across the investment industry, from the Wall Street hedge funds that develop such products to the independent advisors who offer them to clients. Using a strategy called long-short tax-aware, investors use leverage to make hundreds, even thousands, of additional bets on stocks—both those they already own and those they are shorting (shorting is selling an asset in the hopes of buying it back later at a lower price). This new approach intentionally creates losses on both long and short positions, which investors can use to offset gains made elsewhere while preserving the majority of their investment in a rising stock market.

Today, assets invested in "tax-adjusted long-short" are rapidly approaching $200 billion (up from a few billion just a few years ago). Greenwich-based AQR Capital Management, a hedge fund and asset manager with approximately $260 billion under management, was one of the first to employ this exchange-traded strategy. Its success has led to the emergence of blue-chip competitors, including major asset managers BlackRock, Nuveen, and Franklin Templeton, as well as their industry peers, quant hedge funds such as Two Sigma and WorldQuant. All have already implemented products similar to the "tax-adjusted long-short" strategy or are considering launching them. However, AQR's main competitor is the little-known New York-based Quantinno Capital Management. In fact, its founder, 57-year-old economics PhD candidate Hoon Kim, helped create one of AQR's first long-short funds while working for the company for 12 years.

According to the company's website and public filings, as of March 2026, Quantinno had $48.4 billion in assets, spread across 10,600 individual accounts (compared to less than $300 million five years ago). That figure has been removed from Quantinno's website, but in less than six months, assets have grown to approximately $70 billion, according to a wealth management consultant who has seen the company's documents. "I'd say it's a phenomenal success. I've never seen anything like it," said Brent Sullivan, a tax analyst and founder of the blog Tax Alpha Insider.

In March, Sullivan estimated the net assets of the long-short tax-adjusted strategy at approximately $150 billion, spread across separately managed accounts (SMAs), but now, in his opinion, the four largest players account for $170 billion: AQR and Quantinno lead, followed by Gotham Funds and Aperio (a BlackRock division). Meanwhile, smaller players are dreaming of joining the next wave of gold rush. Quantinno and AQR are in a race to dominate this market, each with approximately $70 billion in SMAs. "They're significantly ahead of everyone else right now," Sullivan says.

Kim declined to be interviewed, but it's clear that his company's explosive growth is being fueled by wealth managers whose clients rely on sophisticated tax avoidance techniques for the massive gains generated by the stock market boom. Kim actually founded Quantinno in 2018 as a hedge fund with a tax-adjusted long-short strategy, but quickly shifted his investment strategy to SMAs. This product is the mainstay for hundreds of thousands of wealth managers who, in turn, serve wealthy clients. Unlike traditional mutual funds or exchange-traded funds (ETFs), SMAs can be tailored to a client's preferences and goals. It's estimated that retail segregated accounts currently hold $4 trillion.

"We know how to tailor any strategy to any client," Kim said on the Prime Alpha podcast in 2021.

Kim, a wildly successful analyst, shuns the media. He lives in a $2 million house in the Westchester suburb of Scarsdale, New York, and Quantinno's meteoric success has propelled him into the ranks of self-made billionaires on Wall Street. He owns a controlling stake in Quantinno, which Forbes estimates is now worth over $1 billion, and possibly much more. It all depends on how much a buyer is willing to offer. "There's no known peer group that can compare to Quantinno," says Zach Milam, vice president at Mercer Capital, a consulting firm that specializes in asset manager valuations. "It's a strategic asset in one of the fastest-growing categories in wealth management."

The million-dollar question for Quantinno, AQR, and their imitators is how long the party will last. Two of the largest asset custodians, Fidelity and Schwab (USA), are tightening regulations on long- and short-position accounts, fearing that if investors use leverage too boldly, their own balances will be at risk. After all, these custodians are the ones providing margin loans to investors. As for asset distribution, some advisors are concerned that the strategy is being resold to investors who don't understand it or perhaps don't benefit from it. Moreover, attracting large amounts of borrowed funds for reinvestment in the stock market creates potential risks for the market itself.

"This strategy is untested in a downturn, and the entire category is fraught with risks stemming from tax policy, which can be changed in a jiffy," says Milam. "The asset management industry is replete with examples of companies that rose quickly and then fell even more quickly."

Born in 1968 in South Korea and raised there, Hoon Kim studied business at the prestigious Yonsei University in Seoul before moving to Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, to earn a master's degree and a PhD in economics from Carnegie Mellon University (1996 to 2001). His PhD thesis examined the relationship between accounting, stock valuation, and asset price volatility. His professors included renowned economists and accounting theorists Yuji Ijiri (who died in 2017) and Shyam Sunder. "I remember him! He was very smart and hardworking," says Sunder, 82, a professor at the Yale School of Management (Yale University is considered undesirable in Russia). "He was a student who always had a lot of ideas and was open to new ideas."

In 2001, Kim began his career at Mellon Capital Management, the indexing arm of Mellon Financial (now part of BNY), where he headed up the quantitative equity research department. In 2005, Kim moved to AQR, a hedge fund founded in 1998 by Cliff Asness, David Kabiller, Robert Kreil, and John Lew, who had previously worked under Asness as analysts at Goldman Sachs. Initially structured as a traditional institutional hedge fund, AQR shifted its focus to retail products after the financial crisis. The firm used proprietary computer models to replicate hedge fund returns at a lower cost. They packaged their strategies into mutual funds, which extracted alpha returns through quant analysis, [fueling](https://www.forbes.com/sites/nathanvardi/2017/03/20/how-cliff-asness-became-a-billionaire-by-building-the-vanguard-of-hedge-funds/) rapid growth.

By 2012, Kim was helping manage AQR's global stock-picking team. That same year, he helped launch AQR's family of mutual funds, which implemented a defensive stock strategy: three funds focused exclusively on buying to minimize risk. In 2016, Kim became a portfolio manager for AQR's long-short fund, an open-ended mutual fund. That same year, Kim helped launch a new hedge fund, one of AQR's first funds, which employed a tax-adjusted long-short strategy. "It was a great idea because, if you think about it, we can leverage tax losses in any market situation," Kim shared on a podcast, explaining how this strategy works. "It doesn't matter whether the stock is making money or losing money."

Kim left AQR in early 2018 and founded Quantinno that same year. He recruited two colleagues from his former firm (from the global stock-picking team)—Paul Giordano and Albert Kim—as well as business development ace Glenn Shirley for investor relations and Todd Saunders, a 30-year hedge fund veteran and colleague from Mellon Capital Management. These four joined Kim in 2018 as senior partners and received minority stakes in his business. Tishman Capital Partners, a family-owned investment firm owned by the New York real estate developer of the same name, became Kim's first anchor investor, committing approximately $70 million to Quantinno (according to filings).

However, Kim quickly realized that the main potential lay in the untapped pool of registered investment advisers (RIAs) and wealth managers, which Quantinno could attract with its customizable SMAs. In 2021, Quantinno launched its own DEALS platform, which allows advisers to easily access their accounts with long and short positions. Around the same time, Fidelity Investments, the largest US brokerage, agreed to open its first account managed under a tax-adjusted long-short strategy. This [was reportedly done](https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2026-fidelity-charles-schwab-tax-aware-investing/) at the urging of one of Quantinno's clients (who was also a Fidelity client). Fidelity then lowered the minimum investment thresholds for this strategy, attracting an influx of third-party advisers eager to help their clients defer taxes. In 2022, AQR followed Quantinno's lead by launching its own long-short SMA platform, Flex, aimed at RIAs. Currently, the minimum investor threshold for both companies is $1 million.

"The SMA scheme has really piqued the interest of consultants," comments tax analyst Sullivan. "They're counting on mass customization, and the SMA 'wrapper' allows for this."

Tax-loss accounting is typically straightforward. An investor sells a depreciated stock, takes the loss, and buys another, but not identical, asset. The loss can then be used to offset gains made on other assets. Over time, finding losses in a portfolio with only long positions becomes increasingly difficult: losing stocks are sold, leaving only winning ones. Industry insiders call this phenomenon ossification, and it became a common problem during the nearly four-year stock market boom.

Imagine you're an Nvidia employee with a $10 million stake in stock at a very low cost. You're looking to cash out but don't want to pay high capital gains taxes and still want to reap the benefits of compounding market growth. This is where a long-short SMA comes in handy. In a popular 130/30 portfolio, your $10 million Nvidia position serves as both initial capital and collateral. The manager uses margin to purchase an additional $3 million in long positions and separately opens $3 million in short positions in other stocks. This results in approximately $13 million in long positions and $3 million in short positions, or $10 million in net market value. Losses incurred on both sets of positions can then be used to offset gains as the Nvidia shares are gradually sold. These losses can also help if you have other unrealized gains in your portfolio that need to be withdrawn, such as private company stocks or mutual funds in your brokerage account.

More aggressive investors can explore other options: the 200/100 strategy increases exposure to $20 million long and $10 million short, while the 300/200 strategy increases exposure to $30 million long and $20 million short, representing five times the initial capital in gross exposure. However, these strategies require higher minimum deposits.

The ideal final stage of this strategy is the death of the investor and the transfer of their account to their heirs. Under a tax provision commonly known as "restatement of basis," inherited property typically has a basis equal to its fair market value at the time of the owner's death. Therefore, any increase in value during the original owner's lifetime is generally not subject to capital gains tax. For a long-short account, taxes can be deferred not only on the original collateral (in our example, the Nvidia stock) but also on the appreciation of the shares acquired with leverage.

The caveat is that without such a price base increase, a long-short strategy merely delays capital gains taxes, not eliminates them (AQR founder Cliff Asness [emphasizes](https://x.com/CliffordAsness/status/2084401121087660473) the importance of this point). After all, the main idea of ​​a long-short strategy is to offset taxes while simultaneously earning more on the remaining portion of the portfolio. However, if an investor suddenly needs the money, they will have to liquidate their assets early and recognize taxable income. "You're signing up for one of the longest-term strategies you'll ever encounter," explains Aaron Brachman, a consultant at Steward Partners (Washington), which manages $1.4 billion in assets. He recommends that his clients open long-short accounts only if they are prepared to hold them for life.

In other words, "You're more connected to your managers than you are by marriage," says Matthew Lusins, a consultant in Jackson Hole, Wyoming, who formerly worked at Jim Simons' family office. "You can't just buy 2,000 positions and accounts and then move them somewhere else."

Let's also not forget about fees. In addition to the advisor's fee (for example, Quantinno charges around 0.45% of the asset value), the investor pays the asset custodian, typically between 50 and 150 basis points (or 0.5-1.5% of the asset value), and also incurs transaction costs, which are small but add up to a significant amount, as the portfolio is constantly rebalanced. These expenses can reach 3% of the asset value under management, forcing firms like Quantinno and AQR to consider how to achieve pre-tax alpha returns even during the stock selection stage.

"We need to figure out how to show higher returns just to cover these costs," explains Lusins, who enjoys using the long-short strategy with some clients, but is certain it's not suitable for everyone. "I'm concerned about people's rush into this space. That's the bane of innovative strategies. People sign up for them, and the returns fall. Many later regret their decision."

How Quantinno moves forward will determine whether demand from wealthy investors outweighs the risk controls of the firms managing their accounts, and whether its effective strategy can survive a bear market.

Over the past year, two of the largest American securities custody providers have shown up to this party without an invitation. Fidelity, which helped spark the long-short SMA boom, rejected most new account applications in December 2025, and earlier that year raised fees for some existing clients. This company provides secure custody and asset clearing services for more than 3,400 advisory firms, and as of March, it had custodyed 52% of Quantinno's client assets.

Schwab, which entered the market only in 2025, already held 32% of Quantinno's assets as of March. Schwab provides safekeeping services to over 16,000 registered investment advisors and, like Fidelity, has tightened its regulations in recent months. New accounts were limited to 200% leverage for long positions and 100% leverage for short positions, and each advisor's share of the strategy was capped at 30% of total assets held by Schwab.

If Quantinno decides to look for new custodians, there are options. One of them is Pershing Advisor Solutions LLC, a subsidiary of BNY, which held 13% of Quantinno's assets in custody as of March. Also on the list are Interactive Brokers, Apex Fintech Solutions, and Goldman Sachs, which recently entered the asset custody market using a long-short strategy.

Of course, a rapid and deep market crash will inevitably impact the value of accounts with both long and short positions. However, advisors who agreed to discuss this with Forbes are confident that Quantinno will be able to manage leverage and will be more willing to tailor its plans to the needs of individual clients.


"They've met my expectations from the very beginning," says one satisfied client, Matt Fitzsimmons, managing partner of the $700 million Watchman Group, a Plano, Texas-based firm with clients nationwide.

Advisors like Fitzsimmons are Quantinno's key drivers for the future. If the company can retain its advisors, it will continue to grow. After all, the demand for tax optimization will never dry up, even if it means doing something with your money you don't fully understand. "I don't think anyone questions [long-short strategies], at least our clients don't," says Fitzsimmons. "Even if they have $10 million, that doesn't mean they have to be a master of shorting assets."
